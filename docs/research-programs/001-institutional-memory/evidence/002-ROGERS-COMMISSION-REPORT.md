## Observed Evidence

### CH5-E01 — Launch decisionmakers lacked material O-ring knowledge

**Observation**

The Commission states that those who made the Challenger launch decision were
unaware of the recent history of O-ring and joint problems, unaware of the
contractor's initial written recommendation against launch below 53 degrees
Fahrenheit, and unaware of the continuing opposition of Thiokol engineers after
management reversed its position.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter V
- Printed page 82
- `v1ch5.htm`

**Evidence type**

Commission finding / summary of decision state

**Interpretation status**

Not assigned in Pass 1.

---

### CH5-E02 — The Commission identified failures in communication

**Observation**

The Commission states that testimony revealed communication failures that
resulted in the launch decision being based on incomplete and sometimes
misleading information. It also identified conflict between engineering data
and management judgments and a NASA management structure that allowed internal
flight-safety problems to bypass key Shuttle managers.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter V
- Printed page 82
- `v1ch5.htm`

**Evidence type**

Commission finding / organizational process evidence

**Interpretation status**

Not assigned in Pass 1.

### CH5-E03 — Lower-level technical concerns were not adequately communicated upward

**Observation**

The Commission states that relevant concerns held by Level III NASA personnel
and contractors were not adequately communicated to the Level I and Level II
management responsible for launch. The concerns included Thiokol engineers'
objections regarding low-temperature effects on the Solid Rocket Motor joint
seal and the degree of concern about prior joint-seal erosion.

**Observation**

Thiokol's engineering presentation concluded that the Shuttle should not be

## Qualifying and Contradictory Evidence

### CH5-Q01 — The engineering temperature relationship was disputed

The record includes disagreement over whether the available data demonstrated
a reliable temperature relationship. Boisjoly testified that he could not
quantify his concern from the available data, while he argued that prior
flight experience nevertheless indicated lower temperature was relevant.

This evidence prevents Pass 1 from treating every technical relationship
asserted during the teleconference as independently established.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter V
- Printed pages 89–90
- `v1ch5.htm`

---

### CH5-Q02 — Marshall participants disputed directing the Thiokol reversal

The record includes testimony denying that Marshall ordered Thiokol to reverse
its recommendation. The record also states that Thiokol requested its own
off-net caucus.

This evidence must be preserved separately from the Commission's later
conclusion that Thiokol management reversed its position at Marshall's urging.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter V
- Printed pages 92–100
- `v1ch5.htm`

---

### CH5-Q03 — Existing formal launch criteria did not contain the proposed 53-degree restriction

Marshall's argument included the position that no existing Launch Commit
Criterion prohibited launch based on joint temperature and that 24 previous
missions had flown under the existing criteria.

This does not resolve the safety question, but it is relevant to understanding
the management rationale and must not be omitted from the evidence record.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter V
- Printed pages 95–96
- `v1ch5.htm`

---

### CH5-Q04 — Perception of the first Thiokol communication was not uniform

Lovingood testified that the first teleconference sounded to him like Thiokol
was recommending delay. Reinartz testified that he did not perceive the
communication in the same way.

This difference is evidence that message reception and interpretation varied
even before the formal recommendation was reversed.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter V
- Printed pages 87–88
- `v1ch5.htm`

## Chapter VI — Pass 2 Observed Evidence

### CH6-E01 — The Commission traced the joint problem across the program's history

**Observation**

At the opening of Chapter VI, the Commission characterizes the Solid Rocket
Booster joint problem as originating in faulty design and worsening as NASA and
contractor management first failed to recognize the problem, then failed to
correct it, and finally treated it as an acceptable flight risk.

The Commission further states that as test and flight damage increased in
number and severity, NASA minimized the problem in management briefings and
reports and the amount of damage regarded as acceptable increased.

**Source**

- Printed page 120
- `v1ch6.htm`

### CH6-E02 — Marshall engineers documented serious joint-design objections in 1977–1978

**Observation**

Following early testing that demonstrated joint rotation contrary to original
design expectations, Marshall engineers produced written objections describing
the design as unacceptable and recommending redesign.

The record includes warnings that joint movement could cause seal leakage,
O-ring extrusion, loss of secondary sealing, and potentially catastrophic hot
gas leakage.

### CH6-E09 — O-ring damage became an explicitly accepted flight condition

**Observation**

Following STS 51-C, Thiokol's detailed analysis identified low temperature as
increasing the probability of blow-by and stated that a subsequent flight
could exhibit the same behavior.

Nevertheless, the condition was characterized as undesirable but acceptable.

At the subsequent Level I Flight Readiness Review, the detailed analysis was
reduced to a shorter presentation describing the condition as acceptable risk
because of limited exposure and redundancy, with no temperature discussion
identified by the Commission in that Level I record.

### CH6-E10 — A launch constraint remained open and was repeatedly waived

underlying problem remained under review.

## Chapter VI Qualifying and Contradictory Evidence

### CH6-Q01 — Early Thiokol engineers did not initially consider joint rotation proven dangerous

After the 1977 hydroburst findings were reported to Marshall, Thiokol engineers
did not initially believe the results established that joint rotation would
cause a significant problem and did not schedule additional tests solely to
confirm or disprove the observed gap behavior.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter VI
- Printed page 123
- `v1ch6.htm`

---

### CH6-Q02 — NASA's formal verification process accepted the design

Although the Verification/Certification Committee raised significant
concerns, NASA's program response stated that existing and planned testing
satisfied the intent of the committee recommendations and that the joint had
been sufficiently verified.

The Solid Rocket Motor certification was subsequently deemed satisfactory.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter VI
- Printed pages 125–126
- `v1ch6.htm`

---

### CH6-Q03 — Managers possessed technical rationale for continued flight

The record contains engineering and management analyses supporting retention of
the joint, including arguments concerning primary-seal capability, testing,
Titan experience, expected erosion limits, and conditions under which the
secondary seal was believed available.

These rationales must be evaluated rather than treating continued flight as
evidence that management possessed no technical basis for its decisions.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter VI
- Printed pages 126–139
- `v1ch6.htm`

---

### CH6-Q04 — Marshall repeatedly sought additional investigation and corrective action

Chapter VI documents Marshall engineers and managers identifying defects,
requesting redesign or testing, creating problem records, directing formal
reviews, imposing a launch constraint, requesting corrective plans, and
pressing Thiokol for additional work.

The historical record therefore does not support a simple claim that Marshall
or NASA uniformly ignored the O-ring problem.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter VI
- Printed pages 123–140
- `v1ch6.htm`

---

### CH6-Q05 — Knowledge distribution and receipt were inconsistent rather than uniformly absent

Some Thiokol personnel testified that they were unaware of particular
criticality changes or constraints, while documentary distribution evidence
indicated that Thiokol personnel received or participated in portions of those
processes.

The evidence therefore supports investigating differences between possession,
receipt, understanding, classification, and operational use of information.

**Source**

- Volume I, Chapter VI
- Printed pages approximately 128–139
- `v1ch6.htm`

## Chapter VI Pass 2 Boundary

Chapter VI strongly extends the chronological evidence base beyond the
January 27–28 launch decision.

It does not yet establish that:

- Challenger was caused by an Institutional Memory failure;
- the observed history constitutes normalization of deviance as a completed
  Institute finding;
- information was intentionally suppressed;
- every management decision was unreasonable when made;
- organizational pressure was the dominant causal mechanism;
- knowledge preservation alone would have prevented 51-L;
- the same mechanism explains Columbia;
- any proposition is ready for framework or Canon promotion.

Pass 2 establishes evidence for later interpretation.

It does not perform that interpretation.

Additional primary-source review remains required.

## Chapter VII Pass 3 Boundary

Chapter VII adds substantial evidence concerning safety oversight,
organizational independence, reporting structure, hazard visibility, trend
analysis, staffing, operational tempo, and cross-level communication.

It does not yet establish that:

- Challenger was caused by a single institutional-memory mechanism;
- safety-program weakness alone caused 51-L;
- all safety personnel failed in their responsibilities;
- all relevant information was available to every safety organization;
- staffing reduction was the dominant cause of failure;
- reporting-system changes were intentionally designed to suppress risk;

- the same organizational mechanism explains Columbia;
- any proposition is ready for framework or Canon promotion.

Chapter VII evidence must be interpreted together with Chapters V and VI and
with additional primary-source review.

It does not establish the final case-study finding.

## Chapter VIII Pass 4 Boundary

Chapter VIII establishes substantial evidence that the Shuttle program
operated under increasing schedule, workload, resource, customer, budget, and
institutional pressures.

It also establishes several mechanisms through which those conditions could
affect organizational performance:

- reduced resource margin;
- competing priorities;
- compressed training;
- staff overload;
- reduced learning time between missions;
- late manifest changes;
- inadequate logistics;
- resource diversion toward immediate problems;
- safety effects in other program decisions.

It does not yet establish that:

- outside political pressure caused the 51-L launch;
- President Reagan or the White House pressured NASA to launch Challenger;
- customer pressure directly caused the January 27 recommendation reversal;
- schedule pressure was the sole or dominant cause of the accident;
- every NASA organization subordinated safety to schedule;
- institutional pressure alone explains the failures documented in Chapters V,
  VI, and VII;
- normalization of deviance has been established as an Institute finding;
- any proposition is ready for framework or Canon promotion.

Pass 4 records the pressure environment and documented mechanisms.

Causal synthesis remains deferred.

## Chapter IV Pass 6 Boundary

Chapter IV establishes the physical causal chain.

The evidence now supports the following technical sequence:

right Solid Rocket Motor aft field-joint sealing failure
→ hot-gas blow-by / seal degradation
→ visible aft-joint leakage
→ hot-gas plume
→ damage near the right booster / External Tank aft attachment
→ External Tank structural failure
→ loss of vehicle structural integrity.

Chapter IV also establishes that low temperature materially degraded O-ring
response and that the joint design was unacceptably sensitive to multiple
interacting physical variables.

Pass 6 does not convert those technical facts into an organizational finding.

Technical causation is now anchored.

Organizational causal synthesis remains deferred.

## Chapter IX Pass 7 Boundary

Chapter IX does not extend the physical causal chain of Challenger.

Its value is comparative.

It documents additional Shuttle safety domains in which the organization dealt
with:

- explicit design assumptions;
- known catastrophic failure modes;
- limited safety margins;
- operational experience that differed from qualification expectations;
- corrective actions;
- resource constraints;
- inspection waivers;
- documentation weaknesses;
- reduced independent inspection;
- repeated safety review.

It also provides important counterevidence by documenting cases in which NASA
recognized hazards, changed operations, conducted reviews, funded or pursued
corrective work, and adopted more conservative practices.

The Chapter IX record therefore must not be used to claim that every Shuttle
safety problem shared one causal mechanism.

Chapter-by-chapter Rogers Commission extraction ends with Pass 7.

The next research state is cross-chapter synthesis.
