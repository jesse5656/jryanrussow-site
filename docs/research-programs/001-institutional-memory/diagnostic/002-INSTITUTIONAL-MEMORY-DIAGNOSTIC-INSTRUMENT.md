# 1. Purpose

This instrument operationalizes the twelve candidate diagnostic domains of the
Institutional Memory Diagnostic into:

- evidence questions;
- interview questions;
- analytical tests; and
- finding criteria.

It is intended for applied testing during the first Institutional Memory
Diagnostic pilot.

This instrument does not represent the Institutional Memory framework,
Selective Conversion Failure, the twelve diagnostic domains, or the candidate
conversion chain as universally validated.

The instrument is a development methodology subject to field validation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 2. Diagnostic Boundary

The Diagnostic examines whether materially relevant organizational knowledge
is converted reliably into:

- institutional possession;
- preserved meaning;
- decision availability;
- recognition;
- escalation;
- corrective action;
- execution;
- verification;
- reassessment;
- effective formal control; and
- sustained organizational capacity.

The Diagnostic is not merely a documentation review.

The existence of a document, policy, database, meeting, audit, training
program, corrective-action system, or management process does not by itself
establish that the corresponding organizational function is effective.

Likewise, the absence of a document does not by itself establish that relevant
knowledge does not exist.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 3. Evidence Discipline

Every material conclusion developed with this instrument must distinguish
among:

1. documentary fact;
2. interview testimony;
3. organizational record;
4. corroborated multi-source evidence;
5. Institute inference;
6. unresolved contradiction;
7. hypothesis requiring further investigation.

Evidence and interpretation must not be silently merged.

Contradictory evidence must be retained.

Where evidence supports more than one plausible explanation, the competing
explanations must remain visible until they can be resolved or expressly left
unresolved.

The current instrument does not establish a final evidence-confidence scoring
system.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 4. Analytical Unit

The principal analytical unit is a consequential item of organizational
knowledge.

Examples may include:

- a recurring defect;
- customer complaint;
- safety concern;
- near miss;
- incident;
- audit finding;
- maintenance problem;
- operational abnormality;
- project failure;
- quality problem;
- employee warning;
- management concern;
- corrective action;
- or other materially relevant signal.

For each selected knowledge item, the analyst should attempt to reconstruct
its organizational path.

Candidate analytical path:

**Knowledge exists**

↓

**Knowledge enters institutional possession**

↓

**Meaning and context are preserved**

↓

**Information reaches appropriate authority**

↓

**Significance is recognized**

↓

**Action is authorized**

↓

**Action is performed**

↓

**Effectiveness is verified**

↓

**Experience updates organizational memory and future control**

Failure at one boundary does not establish failure at every other boundary.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 5. Core Trace Record

For each material knowledge item examined, create a trace record containing,
where available:

- trace identifier;
- subject or knowledge item;
- originating event or condition;
- date or time period;
- originating person, function, or system;
- documentary sources;
- organizational systems containing the information;
- relevant recipients;
- decision authority;
- escalation events;
- corrective actions;
- execution evidence;
- verification evidence;
- recurrence or reassessment evidence;
- contradictory evidence;
- affected diagnostic domains;
- unresolved questions;
- and analyst notes.

The trace record is an analytical aid.

Its structure remains subject to pilot validation.

# 6. Domain D1 — Knowledge Existence

## Objective

Determine whether materially relevant knowledge exists somewhere within or
available to the organization.

## Evidence Questions

1. What material problems, risks, abnormalities, defects, complaints, warnings,
   or recurring conditions are documented?
2. What did the organization know before the selected event, decision, or
   failure?
3. What relevant information existed outside the formal system?
4. Were similar conditions previously observed?
5. Did external sources provide relevant knowledge?
6. Was relevant knowledge distributed across multiple people, records, or
   functions?
7. What evidence indicates that important knowledge did not yet exist?

## Interview Questions

1. When did you first become aware of this issue?
2. How did you learn about it?
3. Who else knew about it?
4. Had anything similar happened before?
5. Was the issue documented anywhere?
6. Was there relevant knowledge that people understood operationally but that
   was not formally recorded?
7. What information was unavailable at the time?

## Analytical Tests

- Search for pre-event or pre-decision evidence.
- Compare formal records with interview testimony.
- Identify repeated observations of the same or related condition.
- Distinguish organizational possession from individual possession.
- Test whether hindsight is being mistaken for pre-existing knowledge.

## Finding Criteria

A D1 finding may be considered when evidence establishes that materially
relevant knowledge existed before the consequential decision or outcome.

A D1 finding must not imply universal individual awareness unless supported.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 7. Domain D2 — Institutional Possession

## Objective

Determine where knowledge is stored, documented, remembered, or embodied and
whether the organization possesses it beyond dependence on one individual.

## Evidence Questions

1. Where is the knowledge recorded?
2. Which systems, departments, teams, or individuals possess it?
3. Is it preserved in an organizationally accessible form?
4. Does the organization depend on particular individuals to retain the
   knowledge?
5. What happens to the knowledge when personnel leave or roles change?
6. Are related records fragmented among systems?
7. Can the organization reconstruct the history of the issue?

## Interview Questions

1. Where would someone look for the history of this problem?
2. Who knows the most about it?
3. What would be lost if that person left tomorrow?
4. Are there multiple systems containing different parts of the history?
5. Which source would you consider authoritative?
6. Can a new employee reconstruct what happened and why?

## Analytical Tests

- Attempt to reconstruct the issue from organizational records without relying
  on a single individual.
- Compare repositories for completeness and consistency.
- Identify undocumented dependency on individual memory.
- Test whether fragmented records can be integrated into a coherent history.

## Finding Criteria

A D2 finding may be considered when materially relevant knowledge exists but
institutional possession is fragile, fragmented, inaccessible, or dependent
on individual memory.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 8. Domain D3 — Meaning Preservation

## Objective

Determine whether severity, uncertainty, context, and operational significance
remain attached to information as it moves through organizational systems.

## Evidence Questions

1. How was the issue originally described?
2. How was it represented in later records?
3. Were severity and uncertainty preserved?
4. Did technical or operational context disappear?
5. Was a warning converted into routine administrative language?
6. Were important qualifiers removed?
7. Did different functions represent the same condition differently?

## Interview Questions

1. What did this issue mean when it was first identified?
2. How serious did you believe it was?
3. How was that seriousness communicated?
4. Did the issue appear different after it entered another department or
   reporting system?
5. What context would someone need to understand why this mattered?

## Analytical Tests

- Compare original evidence with later summaries.
- Trace changes in wording, severity, priority, classification, and context.
- Identify whether operational significance was preserved.
- Test whether uncertainty was converted into unsupported certainty or
  dismissed significance.

## Finding Criteria

A D3 finding may be considered when information remains present but material
meaning, severity, uncertainty, or operational context is degraded,
misrepresented, or lost.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 9. Domain D4 — Decision Availability

## Objective

Determine whether relevant information reaches the people or systems with
authority to act.

## Evidence Questions

1. Who had authority to act?
2. What information reached that authority?
3. When did it arrive?
4. What material information did not arrive?
5. Through what formal and informal channels was information transmitted?
6. Were decision makers dependent on summaries prepared by others?
7. Did organizational boundaries interrupt transmission?

## Interview Questions

1. Who could authorize action?
2. What did that person actually know?
3. How would the issue normally reach them?
4. Did you assume someone else had escalated it?
5. What information would have been necessary for an informed decision?
6. Was relevant information available but difficult to retrieve?

## Analytical Tests

- Identify the actual decision authority.
- Reconstruct the information available to that authority at the relevant
  time.
- Compare organizational possession with decision-level availability.
- Identify transmission gaps, delays, filtering, and boundary failures.

## Finding Criteria

A D4 finding may be considered when materially relevant organizational
knowledge existed but did not reliably become available to appropriate
decision authority.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 10. Domain D5 — Recognition and Interpretation

## Objective

Determine whether receiving authorities understand why the information
matters.

## Evidence Questions

1. How did recipients interpret the information?
2. Was the issue recognized as material?
3. Were repeated signals treated as isolated events?
4. Were inappropriate performance measures used to interpret risk?
5. Was technical significance understood?
6. Did different organizational levels interpret the same evidence
   differently?

## Interview Questions

1. What did you think the information meant at the time?
2. How serious did you believe the issue was?
3. What would have caused you to interpret it differently?
4. Did you view this as an isolated problem or part of a larger pattern?
5. What measures or indicators influenced your interpretation?

## Analytical Tests

- Compare evidence received with actions and contemporaneous interpretation.
- Examine classification and prioritization.
- Test whether available indicators adequately represented the underlying
  condition.
- Identify repeated signals treated as unrelated events.

## Finding Criteria

A D5 finding may be considered when relevant information reached appropriate
authority but its material significance was not adequately recognized or
interpreted.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 11. Domain D6 — Escalation

## Objective

Determine what causes issues to move to higher levels of authority and what
prevents or weakens escalation.

## Evidence Questions

1. What formal escalation rules exist?
2. What actually triggers escalation?
3. Which issues were escalated?
4. Which comparable issues were not?
5. Were escalation thresholds clear?
6. Could personnel bypass normal hierarchy when necessary?
7. Were unresolved issues repeatedly returned to the same level?

## Interview Questions

1. When are you expected to escalate a problem?
2. What happens when you do?
3. What makes people reluctant to escalate?
4. Can you escalate outside the normal chain?
5. What happens when an issue remains unresolved?
6. Can you describe an issue that was successfully escalated?

## Analytical Tests

- Compare formal escalation requirements with actual cases.
- Examine successful and unsuccessful escalation.
- Identify stalled, downgraded, or repeatedly recycled issues.
- Test whether escalation depends excessively on individual persistence.

## Finding Criteria

A D6 finding may be considered when material issues do not reliably reach the
level of authority required for resolution despite organizational awareness.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 12. Domain D7 — Corrective Conversion

## Objective

Determine whether recognized knowledge produces concrete corrective action.

## Evidence Questions

1. What corrective actions were proposed?
2. Which were authorized?
3. Were responsibilities assigned?
4. Were deadlines established?
5. Were actions adequate to the identified problem?
6. Were actions repeatedly deferred, narrowed, or administratively closed?
7. Did recognized problems remain without actionable response?

## Interview Questions

1. What happened after the problem was recognized?
2. Who decided what to do?
3. How was responsibility assigned?
4. Were proposed actions changed or deferred?
5. Did the action address the underlying issue or only the immediate symptom?
6. What prevented action?

## Analytical Tests

- Trace recognized issues into corrective-action systems.
- Compare identified causes with authorized remedies.
- Identify unresolved or repeatedly deferred actions.
- Distinguish activity from corrective conversion.

## Finding Criteria

A D7 finding may be considered when recognized material knowledge does not
reliably produce concrete, appropriately bounded corrective action.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 13. Domain D8 — Execution

## Objective

Determine whether authorized corrective action is actually performed.

## Evidence Questions

1. Was the authorized action completed?
2. What evidence demonstrates completion?
3. Does administrative closure match physical or operational completion?
4. Were partial actions recorded as complete?
5. Were deadlines repeatedly extended?
6. Did implementation differ from authorization?

## Interview Questions

1. Was the action actually completed?
2. How do you know?
3. Who verified physical or operational completion?
4. Were there barriers to implementation?
5. Was the action modified during execution?
6. Can items be closed before work is complete?

## Analytical Tests

- Compare authorization, work records, closure records, and actual condition.
- Sample closed corrective actions.
- Identify discrepancies between recorded and actual completion.
- Test whether completion evidence is independent of administrative status.

## Finding Criteria

A D8 finding may be considered when authorized action is not reliably
performed or when administrative closure materially overstates actual
execution.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 14. Domain D9 — Verification

## Objective

Determine whether the organization verifies that corrective action solved the
underlying problem rather than merely closing an item.

## Evidence Questions

1. What constitutes verification?
2. Who verifies effectiveness?
3. Is verification independent of implementation?
4. Are recurrence data examined?
5. Can actions close without effectiveness testing?
6. What happens when corrective action fails?

## Interview Questions

1. How do you determine whether a corrective action worked?
2. Is completion different from effectiveness?
3. Who performs the verification?
4. What happens if the problem returns?
5. Are failed corrective actions reopened?

## Analytical Tests

- Compare completion evidence with effectiveness evidence.
- Examine recurrence after closure.
- Identify actions closed without outcome verification.
- Test whether verification addresses the underlying failure mechanism.

## Finding Criteria

A D9 finding may be considered when the organization treats completion as
equivalent to effectiveness without sufficient verification.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 15. Domain D10 — Learning / Reassessment

## Objective

Determine whether repeated abnormalities, incidents, near misses, failed
corrections, or recurring problems trigger reassessment of the underlying
system.

## Evidence Questions

1. Which problems recur?
2. Are repeated events connected analytically?
3. What threshold triggers broader reassessment?
4. Are prior incidents incorporated into procedures, training, design, or
   management systems?
5. Do failed corrective actions trigger deeper investigation?
6. Does continued successful operation suppress reassessment?

## Interview Questions

1. How many times can this problem recur before the system itself is
   questioned?
2. Who is responsible for identifying patterns?
3. How are lessons from one event applied elsewhere?
4. Have previous fixes failed?
5. What causes the organization to reconsider its assumptions?

## Analytical Tests

- Construct recurrence histories.
- Compare repeated events for common mechanisms.
- Determine whether recurring abnormalities triggered system-level review.
- Trace lessons into changed procedures, controls, training, design, or
  governance.

## Finding Criteria

A D10 finding may be considered when repeated abnormalities or failed
corrections do not reliably trigger reassessment of underlying assumptions,
systems, or controls.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 16. Domain D11 — Formal-System Effectiveness

## Objective

Determine whether formal systems such as audits, training, safety, quality,
maintenance, reporting, management of change, and assurance function
effectively in practice.

## Evidence Questions

1. What formal systems govern the issue?
2. Were those systems followed?
3. Did the systems detect the relevant problem?
4. Did identified deficiencies lead to correction?
5. Do audits repeatedly identify the same issue?
6. Do formal records accurately represent actual practice?
7. Is independent challenge genuinely independent?

## Interview Questions

1. How does this process work in practice?
2. Where does practice differ from policy?
3. What happens when requirements cannot be met?
4. Are exceptions documented?
5. Which formal systems work particularly well?
6. Which systems generate paperwork without reliable operational control?

## Analytical Tests

- Compare policy with actual operating evidence.
- Examine repeated audit findings.
- Test whether formal control systems independently detect and interrupt
  failures.
- Identify systems that exist formally but fail at implementation,
  follow-through, or verification.
- Preserve examples of formal systems that function effectively.

## Finding Criteria

A D11 finding may be considered when formal control architecture exists but
does not reliably perform its intended operational function.

The existence of isolated failures does not by itself establish systemic
ineffectiveness.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 17. Domain D12 — Organizational Capacity

## Objective

Determine whether schedule, staffing, workload, budget, production pressure,
leadership turnover, organizational instability, or related constraints reduce
the organization's capacity to convert knowledge into action.

## Evidence Questions

1. What staffing and workload conditions existed?
2. What resource constraints affected action?
3. Were important tasks deferred because of competing priorities?
4. Did leadership turnover disrupt continuity?
5. Did production or schedule requirements reduce available margin?
6. Were maintenance, training, investigation, or corrective activities
   displaced?
7. Is there contrary evidence showing adequate capacity or management
   resistance to unsafe constraints?

## Interview Questions

1. What prevents people from addressing known problems?
2. Which work is routinely deferred?
3. How do staffing and workload affect follow-through?
4. What happens when operational and corrective priorities conflict?
5. Has organizational change affected continuity?
6. Can you identify occasions when management protected necessary capacity
   despite competing pressure?

## Analytical Tests

- Compare workload and resources with required control activities.
- Examine deferred maintenance, training, investigations, and corrective work.
- Trace leadership and organizational changes.
- Test whether pressure reduced capacity without assuming that pressure was the
  sole cause of failure.
- Search deliberately for contrary evidence.

## Finding Criteria

A D12 finding may be considered when organizational constraints materially
reduce the capacity available for knowledge conversion or control.

Capacity evidence must not be converted automatically into sole or direct
causation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 18. Cross-Domain Analysis

After domain-level analysis, reconstruct the organizational path of each
material knowledge item.

For each trace, determine whether evidence supports:

- knowledge existence;
- institutional possession;
- preserved meaning;
- decision availability;
- recognition;
- escalation;
- corrective conversion;
- execution;
- verification;
- reassessment;
- formal-system effectiveness;
- and adequate organizational capacity.

Do not assume a linear failure sequence.

Multiple domains may interact.

A later-stage failure does not prove that an earlier-stage failure occurred.

Likewise, successful conversion in one organizational context must be
preserved as counterevidence against claims of universal organizational
incapacity.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 19. Contradictory-Evidence Review

Before a material finding is finalized, the analyst must attempt to defeat or
narrow it.

Ask:

1. What evidence contradicts the finding?
2. Is the finding based on hindsight?
3. Is organizational knowledge being confused with individual knowledge?
4. Is correlation being converted into causation?
5. Is contextual pressure being represented as sole cause?
6. Are different organizational levels being collapsed?
7. Did the formal system work effectively in comparable circumstances?
8. Were genuine corrective actions taken?
9. Is the apparent failure actually a documentation problem rather than an
   operational failure?
10. Does another plausible explanation fit the evidence?
11. Does the finding duplicate another finding?
12. Does the wording exceed the available evidence?

A finding that cannot survive this review must be narrowed, held as a
hypothesis, or rejected.

# 20. Candidate Finding Record

Each material diagnostic finding should be developed using the following
working structure.

## Finding ID

TBD

## Diagnostic Domain

D1–D12, including secondary domains where justified.

## Observed Condition

State what the evidence shows.

## Supporting Evidence

List traceable evidence.

## Contradictory / Qualifying Evidence

Preserve material counterevidence and limitations.

## Organizational Boundary

Identify the relevant:

- team;
- department;
- site;
- function;
- leadership level;
- business unit;
- enterprise level;
- or other supported boundary.

## Conversion Failure

Identify the specific boundary or process that failed.

## Operational Consequence

State the supported consequence.

## Organizational Risk

State the risk created or preserved by the condition.

## Evidence Classification

Use the evidence categories authorized by the specification.

Do not invent numerical confidence.

## Recommended Intervention

Target the specific conversion boundary.

Avoid generic recommendations unless the evidence specifically supports them.

## Verification Requirement

State how the organization could determine whether the intervention actually
corrected the identified failure.

# 21. Comparative Counterevidence

The Diagnostic must deliberately search for examples in which the organization
successfully:

- recognized a material problem;
- preserved its significance;
- escalated it;
- authorized corrective action;
- executed the action;
- verified effectiveness;
- or learned across organizational boundaries.

Successful conversion is not noise.

It is analytically important because it may distinguish:

- universal incapacity;
- domain-specific weakness;
- boundary-specific weakness;
- issue-specific weakness;
- and potentially selective conversion failure.

Selective Conversion Failure remains a supported hypothesis and must not be
reported as an established framework proposition.

# 22. Classification Boundary

This version of the instrument does not authorize:

- numerical organizational scores;
- maturity percentages;
- composite risk scores;
- benchmarking claims;
- certification grades;
- universal diagnostic thresholds;
- or validated framework classifications.

During pilot application, findings may be organized descriptively by:

- diagnostic domain;
- conversion boundary;
- organizational boundary;
- operational consequence;
- evidence class;
- recurrence;
- and remediation priority.

A final classification methodology remains subject to pilot validation.

# 23. Pilot Learning Record

During application, maintain a separate methodology-learning record.

Record:

- questions that produced useful evidence;
- questions that produced ambiguous evidence;
- missing questions;
- overlapping domains;
- domains that could not be distinguished empirically;
- evidence types that were unavailable;
- evidence types that proved especially valuable;
- interview questions requiring revision;
- analytical tests that could not be performed;
- findings that could not be classified cleanly;
- unexpected conversion mechanisms;
- successful counterexamples;
- client misunderstandings;
- time required by each analytical activity;
- and methodology changes proposed for the next engagement.

Pilot observations do not automatically modify the research framework.

Material methodology changes must follow repository governance.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 24. Research Feedback Trigger

Applied work should generate a defined research requirement when it identifies:

- an unsupported assumption;
- a missing diagnostic domain;
- materially overlapping domains;
- an indistinguishable conversion boundary;
- a recurring failure mechanism not represented by the current model;
- a successful organizational counterexample that challenges an existing
  hypothesis;
- falsifying evidence;
- or a technology requirement affecting diagnostic validity.

Research should resume against the defined requirement rather than expanding
without boundary.

# 26. Next Development Gate

This instrument must undergo an internal instrument review before pilot use.

The review should determine whether:

1. every D1–D12 domain has been operationalized;
2. questions remain neutral rather than presupposing failure;
3. analytical tests can distinguish adjacent domains;
4. contradictory evidence is structurally preserved;
5. organizational-level attribution is protected;
6. evidence remains distinguishable from inference;
7. successful conversion is deliberately sought as counterevidence;
8. the instrument avoids unsupported mathematical precision;
9. Selective Conversion Failure remains a hypothesis;
10. the instrument can realistically be used in a bounded approximately
    $10,000 pilot engagement.

No pilot should begin until that review is complete.
